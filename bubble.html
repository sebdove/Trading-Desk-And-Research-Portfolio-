The eight pillars

Each indicator becomes a 0 to 100 score: its percentile against every earlier month (at least ten years of history). A pillar averages its indicators that have data that month, and the bubble score averages the pillars. Indicators that trend upward over decades (margin loans, corporate debt) average their level percentile with their three-year change percentile so the score does not drift up by itself.

Explosive-root test

The Phillips–Shi–Yu backward sup ADF test (2015) asks whether a price series is growing explosively rather than wandering like a random walk. For each month it runs a Dickey–Fuller regression on every window that ends that month and is at least 96 months long, and keeps the highest t-statistic. The 95% critical values come from 2,000 simulated random walks of the same length. It runs on three series: log real price, log price to dividends, and log CAPE. A fast rise in real price alone is momentum; a fast rise in price relative to dividends or earnings is the textbook bubble signature. Explosive falls are excluded by requiring the real price to be above its level a year earlier.

Momentum killers

Bubbles end when something takes away the cheap money or the story. These ten switches are on or off each month; the tool reports the share that are on, because fewer series existed in early years.

Crash-buy score

Six parts, 100 points: depth of the fall from the 24-month high (30), how far the bubble score has come down from its 3-year high (20), fear measured by 21-day volatility (15), credit stress widening and then turning down (10), the Fed or T-bill rate falling from its 12-month high (10), and the S&P closing a month back above its 10-month average after a 15% fall (15). The weights were set by reasoning and checked on 1891–1969; 1970–2023 was kept as a test.

Daily updates and the news tilt

Once a day the app downloads the latest S&P 500, Nasdaq, Russell 2000, equal-weight and VIX closes from Yahoo Finance and sixteen series from FRED, and once a week Shiller's file and FINRA's margin statistics. The current month uses the average of its daily closes so far, as Shiller does. If a source fails, the last good copy is kept. With an Anthropic API key, Claude also searches the news once a day and writes a verdict and a tilt of −2 to +2 for each pillar; the tilt is shown beside the data score and never enters it, because it cannot be tested on history.

Avoiding hindsight

Quarterly Federal Reserve and GDP data are used four to six months after the quarter starts, monthly data one month later, Shiller earnings one quarter later, and FINRA margin data one month later. Trend lines are refitted each month on data up to that month. The three-year outcomes shown in the inspector and history tables are for checking the tool and are never used in a score.

Limits

  • Before 1946 there is no margin, household or corporate-debt data, so early scores rest on valuation, expansion, chasing, credit spreads and trend. The Fed's 1928–29 rate hikes and the brokers' loan boom are not in the data.
  • Equal-weight comparisons start in 2003 and the Nasdaq in 1971, so the benchmark pillar is thin for earlier years.
  • Not every bear market is a bubble bust. Wars, inflation and rate shocks (1917, 1973–74, 1980–82) and the 2020 pandemic hit markets that were not in a bubble zone.
  • Bubble zones can last for years. The late-1990s episode ran 56 months, and Greenspan's "irrational exuberance" warning came three years before the top.
  • Private credit, leveraged ETFs, options volume and IPO counts have no long history here, so they appear only in the news comparison.
  • This is a research tool, not investment advice.
